IBBI briefs · register entry

2026-09-26 · leadership

India's corporate failure machinery is heading into its most consequential year since the code began, and almost nobody outside insolvency circles is watching. The Insolvency and Bankruptcy Board of India — the regulator that polices how failed companies are resolved, and through which homebuyers, employees and small suppliers eventually get paid or don't — has a chairperson whose term structure, court-curtailed powers and pending succession are all live questions at once.

What changed

Ravi Mital has chaired IBBI since October 2021. He is still in office as of September 2026 — his signature continued on gazette notifications as recently as May 2026, and an NCLAT order dated 8 September 2026 still lists him as chairperson (register). But on 8 June 2026, IBBI opened the search for his successor, a process that started unusually early against the incumbent's continued tenure (Business Today, 8 Jun 2026).

That timing matters because the chair's own authority is under revision. On 16 July 2026 the Calcutta High Court held that the IBBI Chairperson cannot act as the Disciplinary Committee under the Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code (LiveLaw, 16 Jul 2026). The Disciplinary Committee is the board's enforcement muscle: it is how insolvency professionals, resolution professionals and information utilities get punished for misconduct. A ruling constraining who can exercise that power — arriving while the person who has held it for five years continues in office without a named successor — is precisely the kind of structural drift the register exists to log.

Add a third thread: Clause 73 of the Corporate Laws (Amendment) Bill, 2026 proposes to make IBBI a statutory valuation authority (Cyril Amarchand Mangaldas, 13 Jul 2026). Valuations decide how much creditors recover in every resolution, so handing IBBI that mandate enlarges the regulator exactly as questions about its governance are in court.

Why it matters

The code is not just a corporate workout lane. Since the 2018 amendment, homebuyers are financial creditors and can trigger insolvency against a defaulting builder. Employees' past dues sit high in the repayment waterfall. When a resolution goes wrong — inflated valuations, conflicted professionals, slow processes — the losses land on precisely these unorganised classes, not on the lenders who negotiate the resolution plan. The referee's competence and independence are consumer-protection issues in all but name (register).

Who is affected

Homebuyers with booked flats, workers with unpaid dues and small operational creditors are the classes the repayment waterfall is supposed to protect first. Their escalation routes run through the machinery under question: a homebuyers' association can trigger an insolvency petition as a financial creditor before the NCLT, file proof of claim with the resolution professional, and lodge a complaint against a misbehaving insolvency professional through IBBI's grievance gateway; employees establish dues the same way and can escalate to the bench, then NCLAT. Every one of those routes assumes a functioning Disciplinary Committee to police the professionals in between — which is exactly the power the Calcutta High Court has just constrained.

What to watch

RegTrac logs these as watchpoints on the IBBI register entry:

  • The succession order: who is appointed, whether the appointment predates or postdates a fix for the Disciplinary Committee ruling, and whether the appointing authority publishes the search criteria.
  • A legislative answer to Calcutta HC: watch for an amendment relocating or reconstituting the Disciplinary Committee.
  • Clause 73's passage: if IBBI becomes the valuation regulator, expect a burst of subordinate legislation — regulations, fee schedules, appeal routes — each of which deserves the same scrutiny the code's first decade mostly escaped.
